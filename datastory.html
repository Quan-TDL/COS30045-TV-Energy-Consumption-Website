Buying your first TV? Start here

Shopping for your first TV can feel overwhelming. Australian shops sell TVs in many different sizes and with different screen types, and it's hard to tell which of these actually matter. If you also want to keep your electricity use down, there's even more to think about.

To make this easier, I looked at government data on TVs that are registered and available for sale in Australia. Instead of guessing, this guide uses that data to show you what is common in the market and how energy use changes between TVs. I've put it in the order I'd make the decision myself: size first, then screen type.

Who is this guide for?

Anyone in Australia who is buying a TV for the first time and wants to feel more confident about their choice before spending money.

What will you learn?

You'll learn which TV sizes are the most common, how much choice you can expect at different sizes, and how screen type relates to energy use once you've picked a size.

What this means for you: you don't have to compare everything at once. Make one decision at a time, starting with size.

1. Find out which TV sizes are common

The first thing to ask is: How common is each size of TV?

This matters because the more TVs there are at a certain size, the more models you can compare, and the easier it is to find a good one. The chart below shows how many TVs are available at each screen size, in inches. The taller the bar, the more TVs there are at that size.

From this data, we can see that TVs are not spread evenly across all sizes. Most of them are clustered around certain sizes, which means you'll have a lot more choice in some sizes than in others.

You'll also notice that some sizes have noticeably fewer TVs than the sizes around them. This doesn't mean you can't buy a TV at those sizes, but it does mean you may have fewer models to choose from and compare.

What this means for you: you'll find the most choice at the sizes where the chart is tallest. If you want a size where the chart is low, expect a smaller selection to compare.

Next: size and energy use

Knowing which sizes are common helps you narrow down your options, but size is only part of the decision. A TV also costs you money every time you turn it on, so it's worth knowing how much energy it uses.

Once you have a size in mind, the next question to ask is:

Does the type of screen change how much energy the TV uses?

Let's compare the main screen types across different TV sizes to find out.

2. Compare screen types and energy use

The second question is: How does screen type affect energy consumption?

The chart below compares the average labelled energy consumption of LCD, LCD (LED) and OLED TVs. To keep the comparison fair, the TVs are split into small (below 43 inches), medium (43 to 65 inches) and large (above 65 inches). Energy consumption is shown in kWh per year, so a lower bar means the TV uses less electricity.

From this data, the clearest pattern is that larger TVs generally use more energy than smaller ones. A bigger screen will usually cost you more to run.

Screen type also makes a difference, but this is where you need to be careful. The different screen types are not spread equally across the sizes, so comparing them across all sizes at once would not be fair. A screen type might look better or worse simply because it is more common in smaller or larger TVs.

For this reason, the data does not show that one screen type is always the most energy efficient. It is also based on averages, and an average can hide differences between individual models.

What this means for you: expect a bigger TV to use more energy, only compare screen types within the same size group, and don't assume that one screen type is automatically the best.

Recommendation

Putting both charts together, here's how I'd approach buying your first TV.

1. Decide on a size that suits you. Think about your space and how you'll use the TV, rather than assuming a bigger TV is automatically better. Bigger screens generally use more energy, so going larger has a running cost as well as a purchase price.

2. Check how much choice you have. If your size is a common one, you'll have plenty of models to compare. If it's one of the less common sizes, you may have fewer options.

3. Compare screen types within that size. Look at LCD, LCD (LED) and OLED TVs that are close to the same size, so the comparison is fair.

4. Check the individual models. Averages can hide big differences, so before you buy, look at the labelled energy consumption of the specific TVs you're considering.

Final takeaway

Choose a suitable TV size first, then compare screen types and individual models before you buy. Size has the strongest link to energy use, and screen type adds extra differences within each size.
